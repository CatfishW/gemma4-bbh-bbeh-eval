# VOLT: Formal Statements and Proofs

Notation. Prompts indexed by `i in {1..M}`; current policy `pi_theta`; rollout
`y ~ pi_theta(.|x_i)`; binary reward `r(x_i, y) in {0,1}`; success probability
`p_i = p_i(theta) = E[r(x_i, y)]`; score `S_i(y) = grad_theta log pi_theta(y|x_i)`;
per-prompt gradient `g_i = grad_theta p_i = E[r S_i]`. `E_i[.]` denotes
expectation over `y ~ pi_theta(.|x_i)`. Completion length `|y|`; expected length
`ell_i = E_i|y|`.

Throughout, "predictable" means measurable with respect to the sigma-algebra
generated by all randomness realized strictly before the quantity is used.

---

## Lemma 1 (signal density)

For any prompt `i` and constant baseline `b`:

(a) `g_i = E_i[(r - b) S_i]` (baseline invariance), since `E_i[S_i] = 0`.

(b) `||g_i|| <= sqrt(p_i (1 - p_i)) * sqrt(E_i ||S_i||^2)`.

(c) With `b = p_i`, `E_i ||(r - p_i) S_i||^2 = E_i[(r - p_i)^2 ||S_i||^2]`, and
if `Cov(r, ||S_i||^2)` is negligible (the standard approximation; Greensmith et
al. 2004), this equals `p_i (1 - p_i) E_i ||S_i||^2` up to that covariance term.

**Proof.** (a) is the standard score identity: `E_i[S_i] = grad E_i[1] = 0`.
For (b) write `g_i = E_i[(r - p_i) S_i]` and apply Cauchy-Schwarz in
`L^2(pi_theta)` coordinatewise and sum:
`||g_i||^2 = sum_d (E[(r - p_i) S_{i,d}])^2 <= sum_d E[(r - p_i)^2] E[S_{i,d}^2]
= Var(r) * E||S_i||^2 = p_i(1-p_i) E||S_i||^2.`
(c) is a direct computation: `E[(r-p_i)^2 | y]` has mean `p_i(1-p_i)` and the
cross-term is the stated covariance. ∎

Interpretation: both the attainable gradient magnitude and the per-rollout
second moment scale with the Bernoulli variance `p_i(1-p_i)`. A prompt the
policy always solves or always fails is (to first order) invisible to the
policy gradient; this is the exact continuous analogue of the "degenerate
group" phenomenon in GRPO.

## Proposition 2 (variance-optimal token allocation)

Fix per-prompt estimators `ghat_i = (1/n_i) sum_{t=1}^{n_i} (r_t - b_i) S_i(y_t)`
with i.i.d. rollouts, predictable baselines, and per-prompt variance
`v_i = tr Var[(r - b_i) S_i]`. Consider the aggregate update
`Delta = sum_i w_i ghat_i` with fixed weights `w_i > 0` (e.g., uniform `1/M`).
Under the generation budget `sum_i n_i ell_i <= B`, the total variance
`sum_i w_i^2 v_i / n_i` is minimized by

    n_i*  =  B * ( w_i sqrt(v_i / ell_i) ) / ( sum_j w_j sqrt(v_j ell_j) ).

If additionally the score second moment grows linearly with expected length,
`E_i ||S_i||^2 ~= kappa ell_i` (a sum of per-token score norms of comparable
magnitude), then by Lemma 1(c) `v_i ~= kappa p_i (1 - p_i) ell_i` and, for
uniform `w_i`,

    n_i*  ∝  sqrt( p_i (1 - p_i) )        — the length factors cancel.

Moreover `n_i*` maximizes the standard one-step expected-improvement lower
bound `eta <grad J, E Delta> - (L eta^2 / 2) E||Delta||^2` over allocations at
fixed step size, because the first term is allocation-invariant (Theorem 1
gives `E Delta = sum_i w_i g_i` for every allocation) and the second decomposes
as `||E Delta||^2 + sum_i w_i^2 v_i / n_i`.

**Proof.** Minimize `f(n) = sum_i a_i / n_i` with `a_i = w_i^2 v_i` subject to
`sum_i c_i n_i = B`, `c_i = ell_i`, over the continuous relaxation `n_i > 0`.
`f` is convex; the Lagrangian `sum_i a_i/n_i + lambda (sum_i c_i n_i - B)` has
stationarity `-a_i / n_i^2 + lambda c_i = 0`, i.e. `n_i = sqrt(a_i / (lambda c_i))`.
Substituting into the budget gives the stated normalization. Uniqueness follows
from strict convexity on the feasible ray. Substituting `v_i = kappa p_i(1-p_i)
ell_i` and `c_i = ell_i`: `n_i ∝ sqrt(p_i(1-p_i) ell_i / ell_i) =
sqrt(p_i(1-p_i))`. ∎

Remarks.
1. The exponent depends on the variance model. If per-rollout variance were
   length-independent (`v_i = sigma_i^2`), the optimum would be Neyman-style
   `n_i ∝ sigma_i / sqrt(ell_i)` — the DUET rule (arXiv:2605.08441). VOLT's
   variance model attributes score mass to tokens, which empirically matches
   policy-gradient practice (token-level losses) and removes the cost term
   from the allocation entirely. The two rules coincide when lengths are equal
   across prompts; the assumption, not the algebra, is the difference, and it
   is stated so it can be tested.
2. In practice `p_i` is unknown; VOLT plugs in the posterior expectation
   `E[p(1-p) | history]` (Section 4 of method.md), which (i) is predictable and
   (ii) inflates the score of under-observed prompts toward the prior mean —
   an automatic optimism-under-uncertainty term. The box constraints
   `n_i in {0} ∪ [1, n_max]` and the exploration floor are handled by
   water-filling on the truncated simplex, which preserves the KKT structure.

## Theorem 1 (unbiasedness under adaptive allocation with predictable baselines)

Let iterations be indexed by `k`, with parameters `theta_k` fixed within
iteration `k`. Let `F_{k-1}` denote the sigma-algebra of everything realized
before iteration `k` (all previous rollouts, rewards, posterior states, and
therefore `theta_k` itself). Let the allocation `n_k = (n_{1,k}, ..., n_{M,k})`
be any `F_{k-1}`-measurable random vector (it may depend on the entire
adaptively collected history, including posterior difficulty estimates), and
let the baselines `b_{i,k}` be `F_{k-1}`-measurable. Conditional on `F_{k-1}`,
rollouts `y_{i,t}, t = 1..n_{i,k}` are drawn independently from
`pi_{theta_k}(.|x_i)`. Define the iteration's update direction

    Delta_k = sum_i sum_{t=1}^{n_{i,k}} ( r(x_i, y_{i,t}) - b_{i,k} ) S_i(y_{i,t}).

Then

    E[ Delta_k | F_{k-1} ]  =  sum_i n_{i,k} * g_i(theta_k),        (i)

a non-negatively weighted combination of the true per-prompt gradients with
predictable weights; in particular `E<Delta_k, grad J_w(theta_k)> >= 0` for the
allocation-weighted objective, and unconditionally

    E[ Delta_k ]  =  E[ sum_i n_{i,k} g_i(theta_k) ].               (ii)

No bias is introduced by the dependence of `n_{i,k}` (or of a within-iteration
stopping rule, see the corollary) on past outcomes.

**Proof.** Condition on `F_{k-1}`. Then `theta_k`, `n_{i,k}`, and `b_{i,k}` are
constants, and the rollouts are i.i.d. from `pi_{theta_k}`. For each `(i, t)`:

    E[ (r - b_{i,k}) S_i(y_{i,t}) | F_{k-1} ]
      = E[ r S_i | F_{k-1} ] - b_{i,k} E[ S_i | F_{k-1} ]
      = g_i(theta_k) - b_{i,k} * 0 = g_i(theta_k),

using the score identity `E[S_i] = 0` and the fact that `b_{i,k}` is a constant
under the conditioning — this is precisely where predictability is used, and
where a within-group empirical baseline fails: there, `b` depends on the same
sample, `E[b S] != b E[S]`. Summing the `n_{i,k}` identical conditional
expectations (a constant number under the conditioning) gives (i); the tower
property gives (ii). ∎

### Corollary 1.1 (sequential stopping within an iteration)

Suppose instead rollouts for the iteration are drawn one at a time, the choice
of which prompt to sample next and whether to stop are measurable with respect
to the within-iteration filtration `G_s` (everything up to and including sample
`s`), the baseline used at step `s+1` is `G_s`-measurable, and the stopping time
`tau` satisfies `E[tau] < ∞`. Then

    E[ sum_{s=1}^{tau} (r_s - b_s) S(y_s) ]  =  E[ sum_{s=1}^{tau} g_{i_s}(theta_k) ],

i.e., the estimator remains an unbiased estimate of a stopped sum of true
gradients. **Proof.** `X_s = (r_s - b_s) S(y_s) - g_{i_s}(theta_k)` is a
martingale-difference sequence with respect to `(G_s)` (each term has zero
conditional mean by the argument of Theorem 1, and is bounded because rewards,
baselines, and per-sequence scores are bounded for a fixed finite-length
policy), so the stopped sum has zero expectation by optional stopping. ∎

### Corollary 1.2 (what breaks without predictability)

(a) *GRPO group mean.* With `b = (1/n) sum_t r_t`, a direct computation gives
`E[ sum_t (r_t - b) S(y_t) ] = (n - 1) g_i`, so the estimator is a *scaled*
gradient in expectation for fixed `n` — but the scaling `(n-1)/n` per sample
means groups of different sizes are inconsistently weighted, `n = 1` yields
exactly zero, and conditioning on group non-degeneracy (dynamic-sampling
filters) turns the scale factor into a data-dependent selection weight.

(b) *Empirical baselines under adaptive stopping.* If the stopping rule and the
baseline share samples (e.g., stop once the running mean stabilizes, then use
that mean as the baseline), the conditional-mean step in Theorem 1 fails:
`E[(r_s - b) S | G_{s-1}] = g - E[b S | G_{s-1}]` with `b` correlated with
`S(y_s)` through `r_s`. Reinforce-Ada's pooled-mean baseline after successive
elimination is of this form; Theorem 1 shows the repair is simply to close the
baseline under `G_{s-1}`.

### Corollary 1.3 (single-rollout and degenerate-group signal)

Under Theorem 1 with `n_{i,k} = 1`, the estimator `(r - b_{i,k}) S_i` is an
unbiased estimate of `g_i`; and if all `n_{i,k}` rollouts of a prompt happen to
be correct while `b_{i,k} < 1`, the summed contribution has conditional mean
`n_{i,k} g_i != 0`. Group-relative baselines produce exactly zero in both
situations. The posterior baseline thus converts (i) singleton probes and (ii)
"lucky/unlucky" homogeneous groups from wasted tokens into consolidation
updates whose magnitude `|r - b_i|` automatically anneals as the posterior
concentrates (`b_i -> p_i`).

## Proposition 3 (variance of the posterior baseline vs. group baselines)

Fix a prompt with success rate `p` and consider estimating `g` from `n`
rollouts. Let `v(b) = E||(r - b) S||^2` denote the per-rollout second moment
with a constant baseline `b`, and assume the weak-correlation approximation of
Lemma 1(c), so `v(b) = (p(1-p) + (p - b)^2) * E||S||^2`.

(a) `v` is minimized at `b = p` (the posterior-consistent choice), with excess
`(p - b)^2 E||S||^2` for any other predictable `b` — the price of a stale or
pooled posterior is quadratic in its error, not a bias.

(b) The leave-one-out (RLOO) estimator with `n >= 2` attains per-rollout
second moment `~ (p(1-p)) (1 + 1/(n-1)) E||S||^2`; the predictable baseline
with `|b - p| <= sqrt(p(1-p)/(n-1))` therefore (weakly) dominates it. With
hierarchical pooling the posterior error after even a handful of observations
of the prompt (plus its task siblings) is typically far inside this radius.

**Proof.** (a) expand `E[(r-b)^2] = p(1-p) + (p-b)^2`. (b) For RLOO,
`A_t = r_t - mean_{s != t} r_s`; `Var(A_t) = p(1-p)(1 + 1/(n-1))` and the
cross-covariances between rollouts add a further positive term to the variance
of the summed estimator; comparing per-rollout second moments under the same
approximation gives the radius. ∎

## Proposition 4 (discounted hierarchical posterior as a difficulty tracker)

Let a prompt's true success rate drift slowly across iterations,
`|p_i(theta_{k+1}) - p_i(theta_k)| <= delta`. The discounted Beta posterior with
discount `gamma` and per-iteration evidence mass `m_k` has effective sample
size `ESS <= m_bar / (1 - gamma)` and its mean `b_{i,k}` satisfies the standard
non-stationary tracking bound: bias from drift is `O(delta / (1 - gamma))` and
sampling standard error is `O(1 / sqrt(ESS))`; choosing `1 - gamma ~
(delta^2 m_bar)^{1/3}` balances the two. Partial pooling replaces the fixed
`Beta(1,1)` anchor with the task-level mean `mu_tau`, which reduces the
zero-evidence prior error from `|p_i - 1/2|` to `|p_i - mu_tau|` — the
between-task variance of BBH/BBEH/USR difficulty is much larger than the
within-task variance, which is the entire empirical case for hierarchy, and is
reported with the experiments.

(Stated without proof; standard exponential-forgetting analysis, e.g.
non-stationary bandits. The constants are not used by the algorithm.)

## What the theory licenses, in one paragraph

Theorem 1 decouples *where compute goes* from *whether the gradient is honest*:
any allocation policy that reads only the past — including one that
aggressively concentrates rollouts by posterior difficulty — leaves the
estimator conditionally unbiased, provided baselines are also closed under the
past. Proposition 2 then says the variance-minimizing allocation under a token
budget is square-root water-filling in the posterior Bernoulli variance, and
Lemma 1 says the prompts thereby de-prioritized were precisely the ones that
could not have moved the policy. GRPO occupies the opposite corner: its
baseline looks at the present (biased scaling, dead degenerate groups) while
its allocation ignores the past (uniform groups). DAPO patches the allocation
by rejection sampling — paying for tokens it discards — and keeps the
present-looking baseline; SPO fixes the baseline but keeps uniform allocation.
VOLT is the closure of both fixes under a single posterior state.
